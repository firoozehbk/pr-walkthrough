Species continuity and Fick's second law

The same mole balance on a small shell gives the continuity equation in every coordinate system; only the face areas change. Insert Fick's first law and switch off bulk flow and reaction, and you have Fick's second law. The second tab solves it numerically in a slab, a long cylinder and a sphere.

Step 1

One balance underlies everything

For species A in a fixed control volume \(V\) bounded by the surface \(S\):

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_V c_A\,dV}_{\text{accumulation}} = \underbrace{-\oint_S \mathbf{N}_A\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS}_{\text{net in through the faces}} + \underbrace{\int_V R_A\,dV}_{\text{generation by reaction}} \]

\(\mathbf{N}_A\) is the total molar flux of A (diffusion plus bulk motion) and \(R_A\) is the molar rate of production per unit volume. The three geometries differ only in the shape of the shell, and therefore in the face areas. The rule is the same in every case:

Rate in across a face = (flux normal to that face) × (area of that face), each evaluated at that face's own position.

What makes the cylinder and the sphere different from the box is that a face area can itself change with position. The flux and the area must then be kept together inside the derivative.

Step 2

Cartesian shell \((\Delta x,\ \Delta y,\ \Delta z)\)

All six faces have constant area, so the rate in or out across each pair is just flux times area:

DirectionInOut
\(x\)\(N_{Ax}\big|_x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\)\(N_{Ax}\big|_{x+\Delta x}\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\)
\(y\)\(N_{Ay}\big|_y\,\Delta x\,\Delta z\)\(N_{Ay}\big|_{y+\Delta y}\,\Delta x\,\Delta z\)
\(z\)\(N_{Az}\big|_z\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\)\(N_{Az}\big|_{z+\Delta z}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\)

Balance on the element:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\,\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} ={}& \Delta y\,\Delta z\big(N_{Ax}|_x - N_{Ax}|_{x+\Delta x}\big) + \Delta x\,\Delta z\big(N_{Ay}|_y - N_{Ay}|_{y+\Delta y}\big) \\ &+ \Delta x\,\Delta y\big(N_{Az}|_z - N_{Az}|_{z+\Delta z}\big) + R_A\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z \end{aligned} \]

Divide by \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\) and let the element shrink to a point. Each bracket becomes minus a partial derivative:

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = -\left(\frac{\partial N_{Ax}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial N_{Ay}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial N_{Az}}{\partial z}\right) + R_A \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{N}_A = R_A \]

Step 3

Cylindrical shell \((\Delta r,\ \Delta\theta,\ \Delta z)\)

Shell volume: \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). The face areas now depend on where the face sits:

FaceAreaNote
\(r\) (curved)\(r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\)grows with \(r\), so the inner and outer faces differ
\(\theta\) (flat radial plane)\(\Delta r\,\Delta z\)constant
\(z\) (annular sector)\(r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta r\)the same for the top and bottom of a given shell

Balance, keeping \(r\) inside the radial term because it is evaluated at a different radius on each face:

\[ \begin{aligned} r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\,\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} ={}& \Delta\theta\,\Delta z\Big[(rN_{Ar})\big|_r - (rN_{Ar})\big|_{r+\Delta r}\Big] + \Delta r\,\Delta z\Big[N_{A\theta}\big|_\theta - N_{A\theta}\big|_{\theta+\Delta\theta}\Big] \\ &+ r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta r\Big[N_{Az}\big|_z - N_{Az}\big|_{z+\Delta z}\Big] + R_A\,r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z \end{aligned} \]

Divide by \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\) and take the limit:

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = -\left[\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(r\,N_{Ar}\big) + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial N_{A\theta}}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial N_{Az}}{\partial z}\right] + R_A \]

The term \(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rN_{Ar})\) is the whole story of why cylinders are different. It is the Cartesian \(\partial N_{Ax}/\partial x\) with the growing face area carried along.

Step 4

Spherical shell \((\Delta r,\ \Delta\theta,\ \Delta\phi)\)

Shell volume: \(r^2\sin\theta\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi\).

FaceAreaWhat varies across the shell
\(r\) (spherical cap)\(r^2\sin\theta\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi\)\(r^2\)
\(\theta\) (cone)\(r\sin\theta\,\Delta r\,\Delta\phi\)\(\sin\theta\)
\(\phi\) (meridian plane)\(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\)nothing

Balance:

\[ \begin{aligned} r^2\sin\theta\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi\,\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} ={}& \sin\theta\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi\Big[(r^2N_{Ar})\big|_r - (r^2N_{Ar})\big|_{r+\Delta r}\Big] \\ &+ r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\phi\Big[(\sin\theta\,N_{A\theta})\big|_\theta - (\sin\theta\,N_{A\theta})\big|_{\theta+\Delta\theta}\Big] \\ &+ r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\Big[N_{A\phi}\big|_\phi - N_{A\phi}\big|_{\phi+\Delta\phi}\Big] + R_A\,r^2\sin\theta\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta\phi \end{aligned} \]

Divide by the shell volume and take the limit:

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = -\left[\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\big(r^2 N_{Ar}\big) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\big(\sin\theta\,N_{A\theta}\big) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial N_{A\phi}}{\partial\phi}\right] + R_A \]

Same pattern again: every coordinate whose face area changes gets its area factor inside the derivative and divided out in front.

Step 5

From continuity to Fick's second law

So far nothing has been said about what \(\mathbf{N}_A\) is. Insert Fick's first law. The total flux is diffusion relative to the mixture plus carriage by the molar-average velocity \(\mathbf{v}\):

\[ \mathbf{N}_A = -D_{AB}\nabla c_A + c_A\mathbf{v} \]

Substitute into \(\partial c_A/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{N}_A = R_A\):

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(c_A\mathbf{v}) = \nabla\cdot(D_{AB}\nabla c_A) + R_A \]

Now apply the three assumptions that define Fick's second law:

  1. No bulk motion (\(\mathbf{v}=0\)), or A dilute in a stagnant medium, so \(c_A\mathbf{v}\to 0\).
  2. No reaction, \(R_A = 0\).
  3. Constant \(D_{AB}\), so it comes outside the divergence.
\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\,\nabla^2 c_A \]

The Laplacian \(\nabla^2 c_A\) is exactly the bracket from each shell balance with \(N = -D_{AB}\,\partial c_A/\partial(\text{coordinate})\) substituted, which is why the geometric factors reappear:

Cartesian

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left(\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial z^2}\right) \]

Cylindrical

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left[\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial\theta^2}+\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial z^2}\right] \]

Spherical

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left[\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial\phi^2}\right] \]

Step 6

The radial-only forms you will actually use

Most problems in this course are one-dimensional. Expanding the radial term gives:

GeometryFick's second law, one coordinate onlyExtra term
Slab (half-thickness \(a\))\(\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\dfrac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial x^2}\)none
Long cylinder (radius \(a\))\(\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left(\dfrac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial r^2}+\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\right)\)\(\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\)
Sphere (radius \(a\))\(\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left(\dfrac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial r^2}+\dfrac{2}{r}\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\right)\)\(\dfrac{2}{r}\dfrac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\)

The extra \(1/r\) and \(2/r\) terms are the area-growth terms from Steps 3 and 4. In general the three cases can be written as one equation with \(m = 0, 1, 2\):

\[ \frac{\partial c_A}{\partial t} = D_{AB}\left(\frac{\partial^2 c_A}{\partial r^2}+\frac{m}{r}\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial r}\right) \]

A useful trick for the sphere: the substitution \(u = r\,c_A\) collapses it to the slab form \(\partial u/\partial t = D_{AB}\,\partial^2 u/\partial r^2\). That is why spherical transient solutions look like slab solutions divided by \(r\).

Two things to carry with you. At steady state these give \(N_{Ar}\,r = \text{const}\) in a cylinder and \(N_{Ar}\,r^2 = \text{const}\) in a sphere: the same number of moles per second crosses every shell. And if the stagnant or dilute assumption is dropped but \(c\) and \(D_{AB}\) stay constant, the equation becomes \(\partial c_A/\partial t + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c_A = D_{AB}\nabla^2 c_A + R_A\), the convection–diffusion equation. Fick's second law is its special case with convection and reaction switched off.

Step 7

Solving it analytically: separation of variables

Take the problem the Simulation tab runs. A body starts at a uniform concentration \(c_{A0}\); at \(t = 0\) its surface is brought to \(c_{A\infty}\) and held there. Make everything dimensionless:

\[ C = \frac{c_A - c_{A\infty}}{c_{A0} - c_{A\infty}}, \qquad \xi = \frac{x}{a}\ \text{or}\ \frac{r}{a}, \qquad \mathrm{Fo} = \frac{D_{AB}\,t}{a^2} \]

so that \(C\) starts at 1 and the surface is held at 0. The three one-dimensional problems are then:

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial \mathrm{Fo}} = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial \xi^2} + \frac{m}{\xi}\frac{\partial C}{\partial \xi}, \qquad C(\xi, 0) = 1, \qquad C(1, \mathrm{Fo}) = 0, \qquad \left.\frac{\partial C}{\partial \xi}\right|_{\xi = 0} = 0 \]

with \(m = 0\) (slab), 1 (long cylinder), 2 (sphere). The surface condition is homogeneous, which is what makes separation of variables work.

Slab, \(m = 0\): the full working

1. Separate. Try a product \(C(\xi, \mathrm{Fo}) = F(\xi)\,G(\mathrm{Fo})\). Substituting and dividing by \(FG\):

\[ \frac{1}{G}\frac{dG}{d\mathrm{Fo}} = \frac{1}{F}\frac{d^2F}{d\xi^2} = -\lambda^2 \]

The left side depends only on \(\mathrm{Fo}\) and the middle only on \(\xi\), so both equal the same constant. It must be negative (written \(-\lambda^2\)) or the solution would grow in time instead of decaying to the surface value.

2. Solve the two ordinary equations.

\[ G = e^{-\lambda^2 \mathrm{Fo}}, \qquad F = A\cos\lambda\xi + B\sin\lambda\xi \]

3. Apply the boundary conditions. Symmetry at the centre, \(F'(0) = 0\), kills the sine term, so \(B = 0\). The surface condition \(F(1) = 0\) requires \(\cos\lambda = 0\), which is only true for particular values of \(\lambda\):

\[ \lambda_n = \frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \]

These are the eigenvalues; \(\cos\lambda_n\xi\) are the eigenfunctions. Each one is a solution that keeps its shape and simply decays, the \(n\)-th one at the rate \(\lambda_n^2\).

4. Superpose and fit the initial condition. The equation is linear, so any sum of these is also a solution:

\[ C = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} A_n \cos(\lambda_n\xi)\, e^{-\lambda_n^2 \mathrm{Fo}} \]

At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0\) this must equal 1 for every \(\xi\). Multiply both sides by \(\cos\lambda_k\xi\) and integrate from 0 to 1. The cosines are orthogonal, \(\int_0^1 \cos\lambda_n\xi\,\cos\lambda_k\xi\,d\xi = \tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{nk}\), so only one term survives:

\[ A_n = 2\int_0^1 \cos(\lambda_n\xi)\,d\xi = \frac{2\sin\lambda_n}{\lambda_n} = \frac{2(-1)^n}{\lambda_n} = \frac{4(-1)^n}{(2n+1)\pi} \]
\[ C(\xi, \mathrm{Fo}) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}\cos\!\left[\frac{(2n+1)\pi\xi}{2}\right]\exp\!\left[-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}{4}\,\mathrm{Fo}\right] \]

5. The quantities you actually want. The volume average \(E = \int_0^1 C\,d\xi\) integrates each cosine to \(\sin\lambda_n/\lambda_n = (-1)^n/\lambda_n\), and the centre value is the series at \(\xi = 0\):

\[ E = \frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)^2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}{4}\,\mathrm{Fo}\right], \qquad C_{\text{centre}} = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(2n+1)^2\pi^2}{4}\,\mathrm{Fo}\right] \]

At \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0\) the \(E\) series sums to \(\frac{8}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8} = 1\), as it must. Because the exponents grow as \((2n+1)^2\), the terms die off very fast: at \(\mathrm{Fo} = 0.1\) the second term is already 1.5% of the first, and the one-term form \(E \approx 0.811\,e^{-2.467\,\mathrm{Fo}}\) is the straight line on the log plot in the Simulation tab.

Long cylinder, \(m = 1\): Bessel functions

The same steps, but the \(\xi\)-equation now carries the \(1/\xi\) term from Step 3:

\[ \frac{d^2F}{d\xi^2} + \frac{1}{\xi}\frac{dF}{d\xi} + \lambda^2 F = 0 \]

This is Bessel's equation of order zero. Its two solutions are \(J_0(\lambda\xi)\) and \(Y_0(\lambda\xi)\); \(Y_0\) is infinite at the axis, so it is discarded, exactly as the sine was for the slab. \(J_0\) plays the role of the cosine: it starts at 1 with zero slope and oscillates with a slowly shrinking amplitude. The surface condition \(F(1) = 0\) requires \(J_0(\lambda) = 0\), whose roots are tabulated rather than given by a formula:

\[ \beta_1 = 2.4048, \quad \beta_2 = 5.5201, \quad \beta_3 = 8.6537, \quad \beta_4 = 11.7915, \ \ldots \qquad (\beta_n \to (n - \tfrac{1}{4})\pi) \]

Orthogonality now carries the weight \(\xi\) (it is the \(r\) in the volume element \(r\,dr\)): \(\int_0^1 \xi\,J_0(\beta_n\xi)\,J_0(\beta_k\xi)\,d\xi = \tfrac{1}{2}J_1^2(\beta_n)\,\delta_{nk}\). With \(\int_0^1 \xi\,J_0(\beta_n\xi)\,d\xi = J_1(\beta_n)/\beta_n\), the coefficients are \(A_n = 2/[\beta_n J_1(\beta_n)]\):

\[ C(\xi, \mathrm{Fo}) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{\beta_n J_1(\beta_n)}\,J_0(\beta_n\xi)\,e^{-\beta_n^2 \mathrm{Fo}}, \qquad E = 2\int_0^1 C\,\xi\,d\xi = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{4}{\beta_n^2}\,e^{-\beta_n^2 \mathrm{Fo}} \]

The first eigenvalue squared, \(\beta_1^2 = 5.783\), is 2.3 times the slab's \(\pi^2/4 = 2.467\): a cylinder of radius \(a\) empties 2.3 times faster than a slab of half-thickness \(a\), because it has twice the surface per unit volume and the extra \(1/r\) term steepens the gradient near the surface.

Sphere, \(m = 2\): back to the slab

Use the substitution from Step 6, \(u = \xi\,C\). Then \(\partial u/\partial \mathrm{Fo} = \partial^2 u/\partial\xi^2\), which is the slab equation, with the conditions \(u(0) = 0\) (since \(C\) is finite at the centre), \(u(1) = 0\), and \(u(\xi, 0) = \xi\). Both ends are now zero, so the eigenfunctions are sines rather than cosines, with \(\lambda_n = n\pi\):

\[ u = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n \sin(n\pi\xi)\,e^{-n^2\pi^2 \mathrm{Fo}}, \qquad B_n = 2\int_0^1 \xi\sin(n\pi\xi)\,d\xi = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi} \]
\[ C(\xi, \mathrm{Fo}) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\,\frac{\sin(n\pi\xi)}{\xi}\,e^{-n^2\pi^2 \mathrm{Fo}}, \qquad E = 3\int_0^1 C\,\xi^2\,d\xi = \frac{6}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}\,e^{-n^2\pi^2 \mathrm{Fo}} \]

At the centre, \(\sin(n\pi\xi)/\xi \to n\pi\), so \(C_{\text{centre}} = 2\sum (-1)^{n+1} e^{-n^2\pi^2\mathrm{Fo}}\). The first eigenvalue squared is now \(\pi^2 = 9.870\), four times the slab's, which is why the sphere's line on the log plot is the steepest.

Every case is a sum of shapes that decay exponentially, the weight in the orthogonality integral is the same area factor that appeared in the shell balance, and after \(\mathrm{Fo} \approx 0.1\) only the first term matters. That last fact is what the Heisler and Gurney–Lurie charts are built on: one straight line per geometry on a semi-log plot.

Short times. For \(\mathrm{Fo} \lesssim 0.05\) the series converge slowly because the change has not yet reached the centre and the body behaves as if it were semi-infinite. There the error-function solution is the practical one: measured inward from the surface, \(C = \operatorname{erf}\!\big[x'/(2\sqrt{D_{AB}t})\big]\), independent of \(a\) and of the geometry. Switch the Simulation tab to the central-core start to see the same series machinery at work with different coefficients \(A_n\).
