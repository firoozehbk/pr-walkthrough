Peng–Robinson Mixture Walkthrough

Peng–Robinson for a binary mixture

Step 1

Pure-component parameters

Calculate a and b for each component from its critical temperature, critical pressure and acentric factor. Only α depends on temperature, so recalculate it whenever T changes.

\[ a_i = 0.45724\,\frac{R^2 T_{c,i}^2}{P_{c,i}}\,\alpha_i \qquad b_i = 0.07780\,\frac{R\,T_{c,i}}{P_{c,i}} \]
\[ \alpha_i = \left[1 + \kappa_i\left(1 - \sqrt{T/T_{c,i}}\right)\right]^2 \qquad \kappa_i = 0.37464 + 1.54226\,\omega_i - 0.26992\,\omega_i^2 \]

With your numbers

R = 83.14 cm³·bar/(mol·K). a is in bar·cm⁶/mol² and b in cm³/mol.

Step 2

Mixing rules

Treat the mixture as one fluid whose a and b are composition-weighted averages. The interaction parameter k12 corrects the attraction between unlike molecules.

\[ a_{mix} = x_1^2 a_1 + 2 x_1 x_2 a_{12} + x_2^2 a_2 \qquad a_{12} = \sqrt{a_1 a_2}\,(1 - k_{12}) \qquad b_{mix} = x_1 b_1 + x_2 b_2 \]

Step 3

Solve the cubic for Z

Form the dimensionless groups A and B, then solve the cubic for the compressibility factor Z. One real root means a single phase. With three real roots, the largest is vapour-like, the smallest is liquid-like, and the middle one has no physical meaning.

\[ A = \frac{a_{mix}\,P}{(RT)^2} \qquad B = \frac{b_{mix}\,P}{RT} \]
\[ Z^3 - (1-B)\,Z^2 + (A - 3B^2 - 2B)\,Z - (AB - B^2 - B^3) = 0 \]

Step 4

Molar volume

Convert Z to a molar volume and compare it with the ideal-gas value to see how far the mixture is from ideal behaviour.

\[ V = \frac{ZRT}{P} \]

Step 5

Fugacity coefficients

The fugacity coefficient of each component follows from Z, A and B. The fugacity is then mole fraction × fugacity coefficient × pressure. These are the quantities needed for vapour–liquid equilibrium, where Ki is the ratio of the liquid to the vapour fugacity coefficient.

\[ \ln\hat{\phi}_i = \frac{b_i}{b_{mix}}(Z-1) - \ln(Z-B) - \frac{A}{2\sqrt{2}\,B}\left[\frac{2\sum_j x_j a_{ij}}{a_{mix}} - \frac{b_i}{b_{mix}}\right]\ln\left[\frac{Z + (1+\sqrt{2})B}{Z + (1-\sqrt{2})B}\right] \]

Application

What fugacity means

Fugacity is the pressure a component effectively exerts once the forces between molecules are accounted for. In an ideal gas it equals the partial pressure. In a real mixture the fugacity coefficient corrects it:

\[ \hat{f}_i = \hat{\phi}_i\,x_i\,P \]

Fugacity decides where a component goes. A component moves from the phase where its fugacity is higher to the phase where it is lower, and equilibrium is reached when the two are equal. A flash calculation uses exactly this condition.

Flash calculation

A feed of composition z enters a vessel at fixed T and P and may split into a liquid (x) and a vapour (y). The flash finds the split at which every component has the same fugacity in both phases. The vapour fraction β comes from the Rachford–Rice equation:

\[ K_i = \frac{y_i}{x_i} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_i^{L}}{\hat{\phi}_i^{V}} \qquad \sum_i \frac{z_i\,(K_i - 1)}{1 + \beta\,(K_i - 1)} = 0 \qquad x_i = \frac{z_i}{1 + \beta\,(K_i - 1)} \qquad y_i = K_i\,x_i \]

The loop is: guess Ki, solve for β, calculate x and y, run steps 2 to 5 for each phase (smallest Z for the liquid, largest Z for the vapour), update Ki, and repeat until Ki stops changing.
